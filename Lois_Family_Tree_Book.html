Chapter 1

The Crossing

Long before any of us were here, there was a tailor in a Russian town called Nikolief, or Nikolaev, who made uniforms for the Tsar’s army. His name was David Moses Ressman, and he would whip his children to say their prayers, then have them help him sew. His wife was Hinda Pessin, daughter of a chazan and shochet, sister of a magid, a storyteller. She came from a learned family.

Hinda was deaf. She had been blinded in one eye as a girl when a kite struck her face during play. As a young woman, Cossacks had clubbed her about the head, and that may have had something to do with the deafness too. But she carried thirteen children, seven of whom lived, and she kept the family together long enough to send them, one by one, across the ocean to a country none of them had ever seen.

During the voyage, the children were passed up over a gate to spend a little time with their grandparents.

The first to make the journey was Ed Ressman, David Moses’s brother, who landed in Chicago, then moved north to Appleton, Wisconsin. Beginning around 1890, he conducted minyans and religious services in his own home. That living-room beginning became part of the story of Moses Montefiore Congregation.

In 1902, Abram and Joseph Ressman crossed with their wives and children. In 1903, the rest came: David Moses and Hinda themselves, with Rachel and her children. The Ressmans traveled tourist class. Rachel and her children traveled steerage. The boat may have been called the Saxonia. It landed in Baltimore. From there, they took the train to Chicago.

They founded a company called Acme Manufacturing that made parts of suits, but there was so much fighting within the family that the business did not last. Every Ressman brother worked in the tailoring trade. Rachel scrubbed floors for fifty cents apiece. They were tired and they were poor and they were here, and that is where this story actually begins.

Story 1

Hinda & the Cossacks

From the Old Country

Hinda Pessin was born into a learned family in Russia. Her father, Harry Pessin, was a chazan and shochet. Her brother Nathan was a magid — a storyteller. Her mother was Esther Skolnick Pessin. They were, by the family's own account, "a well-educated family."

But the world she was born into was not kind to Jewish women, and Hinda's life carried scars from it. She was deaf — a condition some of her children would inherit in lesser degree. She had been blinded in one eye as a child when a kite some children were playing with struck her in the face. And as a young woman, she had been clubbed about the head by Cossacks, which the family always suspected may have contributed to her deafness.

She married a tailor named David Moses Ressman in Nikolief, where he made uniforms for the Tsar's army. They had thirteen children. Only seven survived. And eventually, in 1903, she boarded a boat with the children who had lived and crossed the Atlantic to a country whose language she did not speak.

If you want to know where the family's stubbornness comes from — its refusal to be diminished — it comes from Hinda.

Story 2

Tea From a Saucer

How Rose Met Leon

Rose Vanetsky was the only child of Louis and Machla Vanetsky. Louis was a junk peddler who rode his horse and wagon through the Englewood neighborhood of Chicago, buying and selling. They never had a lot of money, and like many families of the time, they kept a boarder in the house to help pay the rent.

One day when Rose was about sixteen, Leon Ressman — the youngest of David Moses's seven surviving children — came by the house with friends. Rose was sitting on her mother's lap drinking tea out of a saucer. The way the family told it, he wasn't very impressed with her, and thought she acted like a baby.

Still, they started dating. When they were both nineteen, they had a big wedding. In those days, the bride's family did the work — Grandma Vanetsky even plucked all the chickens for the dinner.

That was 1909. Their first child, Sylvia — Lois's mother — was born the next May.

Story 3

The Saxonia

The Crossing, 1903

It happened in two waves. In 1902, Abram Ressman and his wife Celia, and Joseph Ressman and his wife Jennie, with their children, crossed the Atlantic with Rachel's husband David Beilen ahead of her. The next year, in 1903, Rachel followed with the children — and David Moses Ressman and Hinda came too, with the rest of the family.

The Ressmans had managed to afford tourist class. Rachel and her children, however, traveled in steerage, in the cramped lower decks where most immigrants spent the journey.

What the family remembered most about that voyage was a small, tender thing: during the voyage, the children were passed up over a gate to spend a little time with their grandparents. Imagine that scene — the boat rolling, the salt wind, hands reaching down through the bars to lift up a child for a moment.

The boat may have been the Saxonia. It docked in Baltimore. From there, the family rode the train to Chicago, where they would settle, fight, fail in business, succeed in business, and put down the roots that would eventually carry their name into your kitchen in Phoenix.

Story 4

Moses Montefiore Congregation

Appleton, Wisconsin

Edward "Ed" Ressman — David Moses's brother — was the first of the family to come to America. He arrived as a peddler and scrap dealer with his wife Sonya Hirshkovitz. They settled first in Chicago, then moved north to Appleton, Wisconsin, where Sonya had relatives, the Greenbergs.

Appleton, in the late nineteenth century, had a small Jewish community but no synagogue. Ed solved that problem the way the family solved most problems: by doing it himself. Beginning around 1890, he conducted minyans and religious services in his own home. He owned his own Torahs, and the early peddlers, the only other Orthodox Jews of the city, attended.

The Moses Montefiore Congregation was officially founded in 1903. Its first officers included President David Belzer, Vice President Abe Levin, Secretary A. L. Jacobson, and Treasurer Isadore Bahcall — the same Isadore who came to America after Rebecca Ressman gave him her ticket.

In 1910, Aaron Zussman came to Appleton with his family and became cantor, teacher, shochet, and spiritual leader. A synagogue was built on the corner of Atlantic and Bateman streets in 1922 and dedicated on June 10, 1923. The congregation later joined United Synagogues of America in 1965, moved to a modern North Meade Street building in 1970, and continues as part of Jewish life in the Fox Valley.

So the story is larger than one living room. It is Ed's home, Isadore's treasury, a Bateman Street synagogue, and a community that kept adapting for more than a century.

Story 5

Rachel Scrubbed Floors

First Years in Chicago

The Ressmans landed in Baltimore and made their way to Chicago, where the rest of the family had already begun to settle. They were tailors. That's all they had ever been. So they did what tailors do: they founded a company.

It was called Acme Manufacturing, and it made parts of suits. The Ressman brothers — Abram, Louis, Joseph, Harry, Leon — all worked in it. But there was, the family said with characteristic plainness, so much fighting within the family that the business didn't last.

When Acme failed, the brothers scattered. All the Ressman brothers worked in the tailoring trade. Rachel — the eldest — took whatever work she could get, including scrubbing floors for fifty cents.

Harry Ressman went to work for Hart, Schaffner & Marx, a major manufacturer of men's clothing. There he met another employee, Sarah Spector, and married her. On their honeymoon, they visited Harry's sister Rebecca Bahcall in Appleton. They liked it. They had no money and no jobs, but they stayed — and eventually opened a clothing store of their own called Ressman's Clothiers. That store still operates today, run by Harry's grandson Alan Rusky.

Story 6

The Cleaning Plant

Pontiac, Illinois

After Leon and Rose married in 1909 and Sylvia was born the next May, Leon decided to leave Chicago. The labor problems in the clothing industry were too much for him, and he wanted something of his own.

They moved ninety miles south, to a small town called Pontiac, Illinois — the county seat, with about six Jewish families. Leon opened a cleaning and dyeing plant. His first machines were operated by hand. He worked very hard. Eventually he opened a plant on the main street of town, and the business succeeded.

Two and a half years after Sylvia was born came Ruth, then a son, Ezra. The Sacks and the Moshines were the closest families — they got together for every Holiday and weekend.

But by the time Sylvia was about ten, Leon was getting ill from the gasoline fumes he worked with every day. They had to leave. Leon's cousin Joe Bailen suggested Green Bay, Wisconsin, where they could open a clothing store together. So they went.

The Green Bay store failed in the Depression. They came back to Chicago. Leon opened a tailor shop at 3253 W. Montrose Avenue, with the family living in a small apartment behind the store. Sylvia went to high school. Ruth and Ezra to grammar school. Those were struggling years, but we were a very happy family, Sylvia would say later, and never really thought that we were missing very much.

Story 7

Sylvia & the Porch

Lois's Mother

Sylvia Ressman — Lois's mother — was born May 28, 1910 in Pontiac, Illinois, the firstborn of Leon and Rose. By every account, she had a happy childhood. The cleaning plant was doing well. The town was small, the Jewish community close.

One day, when she was about nine, she fell off a second-floor porch. They didn't know until some time later that she had broken her kneecap. She had many operations on that knee, but came through them all.

The ankle gave her trouble, the family said, because she wanted to do the things the other girls did, and used her leg too much. She had a slight limp her whole life. And, the record notes simply: it never stopped her from raising her three children, and doing all the things that a housewife in those days had to do to maintain a busy household.

She had, the family also remembered, a good singing voice. And she was very good at arithmetic.

That toughness — the refusal to let a broken knee define a life — is the same toughness that came through Hinda from Russia, that carried Rose through the Depression, and that you carried into your own life. It runs in the line.

Story 8

Ezra Sent His Pay Home

World War II

Ezra Ressman — Sylvia and Ruth's younger brother — went to the University of Illinois downstate. Ruth and Sylvia worked, and the family helped put their brother through college. Money was hard. These were Depression years.

After his pre-law degree, Ezra continued at DePaul University to study law. He passed the State Bar Exam, became a full-fledged lawyer, and opened a practice with two friends, Mor Levin and Jerry Blonder.

Then the war came.

Ezra went into service. He was drafted, where his partners weren't. While he served, he sent his army pay back home to keep the law office going. His partners maintained the practice in his absence.

After the war, Ezra's story reached unexpectedly west. He was one of the original investors in Ramada, whose first hotel opened on Route 66 in Flagstaff, Arizona in 1954. The family line runs through tailor shops, scrap yards, law offices, and then, somehow, into the motel signs of the American highway.

He never married. He died many years later, having spent his life as a Chicago lawyer — quiet, generous, the kind of man who would mail his army pay back to his partners so the work could go on.

Story 9

Ressman's Clothiers

Appleton, Wisconsin — Still Open

This is one of the great enduring stories of the family. Harry Ressman went to work for Hart, Schaffner & Marx in Chicago after the closing of Acme Manufacturing. There he met another employee, Sarah Spector, and married her.

On their honeymoon, they went to visit Harry's sister Rebecca Bahcall in Appleton, Wisconsin. They liked it so much they decided to stay — with no money and no jobs. Harry started doing tailoring work for the people of the town, and eventually they opened a small clothing store called Ressman's Clothiers.

They had two daughters, Diana (1910) and Esther "Jere" (1914). Diana married Ben Rusky, a salesman, and they had a son, Alan Rusky, born 1942. When Alan grew up, he came to work in the store — and today, more than a century after that honeymoon visit, Alan still owns Ressman's Clothiers. His wife Annette works there too. Their one employee is Jared "Jerry" Bailin, the great-grandson of Harry's sister Rachel.

In 1979, a local newspaper ran a feature with a photograph of Alan Rusky standing in the store, holding up a comically large pair of shorts under the headline "His undies in a bundle over this." The store was thriving even then. It still is.

Story 10

The Playboy Connection

An Unexpected Branch

Of all the threads that ran out of Appleton, Wisconsin and the family scrap business, this one is perhaps the most unexpected.

Rebecca Ressman — David Moses's daughter, the one who gave her ticket to Isadore Bahcall — settled in Appleton with him after he came over with Rose. They had three children: Rose, Edward, and Sarah "Bebe." Rose Bahcall married Walter Rosenzweig from Bessemer, Michigan. Walter went into the scrap business with his father-in-law. They had two children: Donna (1929) and Richard (1935).

Richard Rosenzweig lived in Chicago for many years before moving to Los Angeles. There, he became Executive Vice-President of Playboy Enterprises and President of Playboy Productions — the production company involved in television, film, and (at the time of the family record) "preparing video disc systems." He was married twice — to Elaine Fantle and Bonnie Bovac — and divorced from both.

From a tailor in Russia to the Tsar's army to a scrap heap in Appleton, Wisconsin to the executive suite of Playboy Enterprises, in three generations. Only in America.

Story 11

The Vanetskys

Englewood, Chicago

On your grandmother Rose's side, the story starts in Englewood, on the south side of Chicago, sometime between 1870 and 1880, when Louis and Machla (Mary) Vanetsky arrived from somewhere in Russia. Where exactly, no one wrote down.

Rose was their only child. The family had tried to have other children, but none survived birth or early childhood. So Rose grew up an only child, raised in a small home that almost always had a boarder living in it to help pay the rent. Her mother used to tell her that the boarder was like a big brother to her; many years later, Rose would still know him and his children.

Louis was a junk peddler. He rode his horse and wagon through Englewood, buying scraps from people on the street and selling what he gathered to the junk yards that existed in those days. He was, in Rose's memory, kind of tall, and always wore a mustache and a little Van Dyke beard.

Machla was small and plump — Rose's word — with a good singing voice and a head for arithmetic. The family name Rifkin, her maiden name, would echo down the line. She died after surgery for gallbladder disease while the family was living in Green Bay. Louis came to live with the family in Chicago after that.

This is half of where you come from on your mother's side. Junk and scrap and a horse and wagon and an only daughter who fell off a porch and never let it slow her down.

Story 12

Rachel Beilen

The Eldest, Born 1865

Rachel was the eldest of David Moses and Hinda Ressman's children — born in 1865 in Russia, died in Chicago in 1954 at the age of eighty-nine.

She had married, in Russia, a man named David Beilen, whose father was a farmer and the mayor of Kolonia (or Bohakuv). Her parents had not approved of the match — David was in poor health — but she married him anyway.

David came to America in 1902, a year before Rachel and the children. He went to work for Ed Ressman in Appleton, but didn't like it there, so he moved back to Chicago and worked as a tailor. He went to Denver, hoping the dry air would help his lungs. It didn't. He died there a year later — of tuberculosis of the throat, at the age of thirty-eight.

Rachel was left in Chicago with seven children. They were Esther, Bessie, Eva, Joseph, Bertha, Sadie, and Abram. The youngest, Abram, was the only one born in America. When the children were in school, Joseph decided that the family name Beilen ought to be spelled the way it was pronounced, so the teachers could read it. They became the Bailins, and there are Bailins in this family ever since.

Rachel scrubbed floors for fifty cents apiece. She lived to see grandchildren and great-grandchildren. She is buried in Chicago.
